Replication evidence on Wikipedia: review cards

English Wikipedia articles that cite studies others have tried to replicate, mostly in psychology and the behavioural sciences. Each card asks whether the article should mention the replication, and how. A private prototype, prepared with AI help and not yet checked by people; nothing is posted to Wikipedia.

Before you start

Each card looks at one English Wikipedia article and the studies it cites that others have tried to replicate. Most are findings from psychology and the behavioural sciences, such as priming, default effects or stereotype threat; a few come from economics or biology. The studies and their replications come from FLoRA, the FORRT Library of Reproduction and Replication Attempts. We used AI to read the article and the papers we could get, and to suggest what, if anything, could change.

How to use it: read the card, read the sources, make any edit on Wikipedia itself in another tab, then come back and record what you did.

  • Write in your own words. English Wikipedia bans AI-written article text, and other editors may collapse talk-page comments that are obviously AI-written.
  • The suggestions are AI output, not yet checked by people. You decide what, if anything, to do.
  • "No change needed" is a common, useful answer. A failed replication does not make a sentence wrong: many articles cite a study for its history or already discuss the replication.

Adds a replication (main aim)
Start here. The suggested edit cites a replication that the article does not cite yet. This is what the project is for; the other corrections we noticed along the way are useful but secondary.

Kind of suggestion

Every card also carries one of four labels, saying what the AI suggested:

Edit suggested
The article says something its source does not support, or leaves out a replication that tested a claim it makes.
Judgement call
Either two independent AI assessments disagreed about whether a change is needed, or the AI suggests no change although a replication bears on a claim the article relies on. The card shows both views, or the AI's reasons, for you to weigh. Some also carry Minor notes.
Optional addition
The article is accurate; adding more information is up to you.
No change needed
No edit has a point here: the paper is only listed, mentioned in passing, already reported with its replication, or the replication tests something the article does not rely on. Some of these also carry Minor notes: small things such as a typo in a reference, or material that belongs in another article.

Worth a look shows the first two together, Edit suggested and Judgement call, whether or not the edit would add a replication.

Other labels on the list: Living person and Health (extra care, see later), Edited since (the article changed after our snapshot), Library access (at least one paper has no known free copy; the “Reading the papers” filter hides these), ✓ reviewed (you have saved a decision) and Draft (you have started a review but not saved it).

Reading a card

A card has one section per publication (a paper the article cites; one paper can report several studies). The bar at the top of the card stays in view and jumps to the form for recording your review. Each section has three parts:

Papers line and AI recommendation
The original study and its replications, named by author and year (hover for the full reference), and what the AI suggests. An open padlock after a paper means you can read it for free: gold on the publisher's site, green as a copy elsewhere (click it). No padlock: you may need library access.
A box with three tabs
Comparison: the Wikipedia sentence beside the evidence from the papers, with any questions to decide before acting. Citations are marked [this study], [replication] and [other source]. Papers: each paper, what the AI read of it, where to read it for free (or that you may need library access), and the passages quoted from it. Details & AI review: the points an edit could cover, then each AI statement beside the passage it relied on, and the search of review articles. Papers were read in full unless marked Abstract only or Title and details only; checks marked ✗ Not in the source need your attention.
AI interpretation
Below the box: the AI's reason, and for an edit What, Where and Cite. We deliberately give no ready-made wording: the text is yours to write. For “No change needed”, it says why.

The bar at the very top switches to earlier layouts of the same card, kept for comparison.

Reading a replication

A replication repeats an earlier study to see whether its result holds. What it tells you depends on how it was done.

  • Did it test the same claim? A direct replication repeats the original method as closely as it can; a conceptual one tests the same idea another way. A replication may also cover only one experiment in a paper. Check that it tested the claim the article makes (the card's "How this suggestion was made" section gives the AI's view).
  • One study settles little either way. A failed replication does not show that the original finding was false, and a successful one does not prove it true. Larger samples, preregistered designs and multi-lab projects (such as Many Labs or Registered Replication Reports) carry more weight than a single small study.
  • Mixed results are common. Avoid words such as "debunked" or "confirmed". Say what was done and found, and who did it: "A 2012 replication with 120 participants did not find the effect."
  • Reviews outweigh single studies. One replication can support a short, attributed sentence in an article about the effect. Statements about "the evidence" or whether an effect is real need a review or meta-analysis; the card lists any reviews we found that discuss the replication.
  • Keep due weight. In a broad article, or where the study is mentioned in passing, making the claim more precise often works better than a new paragraph on replication.
  • The database's labels ("failed", "mixed") are screening labels. They help us find cards; they are not citable and are not a verdict.

Checking, deciding and recording

  • Read the sources yourself. Each quoted passage appears word for word in the paper, but the AI may have misread it. Check anything your edit will rely on, and note in your record if something looks wrong.
  • Cards are snapshots. Use "Changes since this snapshot" or "Check now" to see whether someone has already edited the article.
  • Take extra care with living people and health topics. Findings are about studies, not people (WP:BLP); health claims need reviews, not single studies (WP:MEDRS).
  • If you are unsure, raise it on the article's talk page or record "Needs more checking first".
  • Your records stay in this browser (a limitation of this prototype; shared storage is planned). What you type is kept as a draft as you go; a card counts as reviewed only once you click "Save review". Under "Your reviews", at the top right of the page, add your initials and use Export CSV or Export JSON to keep or share your records.
