What these cards are

Each card describes one English Wikipedia article and every study on it that is listed in FLoRA (the FORRT Library of Reproduction and Replication Attempts). We used AI to read the article passage and the sources we could retrieve, and to suggest what, if anything, a careful editor might change. The cards are research material shared for review, and nobody has checked them yet: whether to edit is your decision.

A FLoRA match is a screening signal. The absence of a replication DOI on a page does not show that the article ignores replication evidence, and a failed replication does not by itself make a claim wrong. Many cards therefore recommend no change, and we report those as useful outcomes.

The rule on generated text

English Wikipedia prohibits using large language models to generate or rewrite article content, apart from copyediting your own writing and translation (WP:NOLLM, revision 1378473005, checked 6 October 2026). Persistent use outside those exceptions is treated as disruptive. Talk-page comments that are obviously generated can be collapsed (WP:AITALK, revision 1378840295).

The cards therefore give no ready-made wording. Each suggested edit says where a change could go, which sources it could cite and which points from those sources it could cover; the text is yours to write from the sources you have read. Do not use AI output in an article, an edit summary or a talk page.

Clicking “save” yourself does not turn a generated suggestion into your own work, and making many similar edits from a list can be treated as bot-like editing (WP:MEATBOT). The prototype has no edit, submit or copy-to-Wikipedia function by design.

Working through a card

  1. Open the snapshot and the live article. Use “Changes since this snapshot” to see whether the passage has changed. If it has, re-read the current text; the card describes the snapshot only.
  2. Read the whole section and the talk page, including archives, for earlier discussion of the study or its replications. Existing consensus takes precedence over our suggestions.
  3. Identify the claim. Which sentence does the citation support, and is it presented as current empirical fact, as history (“X first proposed…”), as an example, or only as a bibliography entry? Historical wording that is used to support a present-day claim counts as current support.
  4. Use “What we checked”. We collected the papers we could (open-access copies, author and repository versions), and the AI checked the facts each suggestion relies on against them. Each check shows its verdict and the passage it quotes. The quote appears word for word in the source, which shows that the passage exists, not that the AI understood it, so read the sources yourself and check anything your edit will rely on. If a quote looks wrong or out of context, say so in your notes. Each check also says what was read: the AI relies on an abstract only for what it plainly states (what was tested, in whom, the main result) and gives further detail only where it read the full paper. If neither could be obtained, the card says so.
  5. Weigh the evidence. A single replication is a primary source: it can support a short, attributed, descriptive sentence in an article about that effect, but not a conclusion about “the evidence”. Review articles we found that discuss the replication are listed on the card. See “Reading a replication” below. Do not ask other Wikipedia editors to check sources for you.
  6. Decide on the smallest change that makes the claim accurate. The options are: no change; add context; narrow the wording; replace or upgrade the source; remove an unsupported claim or citation (only where the source does not support it, never merely because a replication failed); or open a talk-page discussion. Deferring is a good choice when you lack source access or subject expertise. Each suggestion carries a strength. A fix means the article says something its cited source contradicts or does not support. A recommended addition means the article relies on the study and leaves out a replication that tested the same claim and failed or clearly qualified it. An optional addition means the article is accurate and adding information is your choice. A judgement call means one of two things: two independent AI assessments disagreed about whether a change is needed (the card shows both views), or the AI suggests no change although a replication bears on a claim the article relies on (the card shows the AI’s reasons for you to weigh). No change needed is kept only where no edit has a point: the paper is only listed, a biography or list mentions it in passing, the article already reports the replication, or the replication tests something the article does not rely on.
  7. Write in your own words, matching the article’s variety of English, tone and citation style. Attribute primary findings in text (“A 2016 preregistered replication by … found …”), avoid weasel words, and keep due weight: a tangential mention usually needs at most a narrower claim, not a new paragraph on replication.
  8. Record your decision for each study on the card (edit, talk page, no change, needs more checking, or skip; give a reason when you disagree with the suggestion). What you enter is kept in your browser as a draft as you go; the card counts as reviewed once you click “Save review”, and cards with an unsaved draft are marked “Draft” in the list. Export your records from “Your reviews” at the top right. The record notes which article version you reviewed. “Clear form” empties the form if you decide not to review the card (“Discard changes” goes back to your saved review). Where shared storage is switched on, sign in under “Your reviews” with your reviewer ID (initials and four digits, such as BS#1312) before saving: saved reviews are then shared, “I’m reviewing this now” marks a card as under review, and two other people audit each review before the card counts as audited.

Reading a replication

A replication repeats an earlier study to see whether its result holds. What it tells you depends on how it was done.

  • Did it test the same claim? A direct replication repeats the original method as closely as it can; a conceptual one tests the same idea another way, and a failure says less about the original study. A replication may also cover only one experiment in a paper. Check that it tested the claim the article makes; the card’s “How this suggestion was made” section gives the AI’s view.
  • One study settles little either way. A failed replication does not show that the original finding was false, and a successful one does not prove it true. Larger samples, preregistered designs and multi-lab projects (such as Many Labs or Registered Replication Reports) carry more weight than a single small study.
  • Mixed results are common. Avoid “debunked” or “confirmed”. Say what was done and found, and who did it: “A 2012 replication with 120 participants did not find the effect.”
  • Reviews outweigh single studies. Statements about “the evidence” or whether an effect is real need a review or meta-analysis.
  • Keep due weight. In a broad article, or where the study is mentioned in passing, a more precise claim often works better than a new paragraph on replication.
  • FLoRA’s outcome labels are screening labels. They are not citable and are not a verdict on the study.

Extra care

  • Living people (WP:BLP). Replication outcomes concern findings, not people. Do not imply misconduct or incompetence unless high-quality sources say so directly, and do not add replication material to a biography unless it is about that person’s work and has secondary coverage.
  • Health and biomedical content (WP:MEDRS). Use recent systematic reviews, meta-analyses and major textbooks. Primary studies and single replications, including Reproducibility Project: Cancer Biology papers, are generally not adequate sources for biomedical claims.
  • Conflicts of interest. If you are an author of a study on the card, or the edit would promote FORRT/FLoRA or your own papers, do not review that card; disclose the connection and use an edit request on the talk page rather than editing directly. Disclose paid or funded editing as the Wikimedia Terms of Use require (WP:COI). Do not cite FLoRA itself as a source.
  • Disagreement. If an edit is reverted or challenged, discuss it on the talk page; do not organise others to restore it.

Limits of the checks

Automatic checks confirm that the passages a card quotes from the article and the sources appear in the saved texts, that every source it cites was among those we collected or is already cited in the article, and that each label for what was read (full text, abstract, or title and details) is right. They do not show that the AI understood the sources, that every relevant review was found, or that the suggestion is what an experienced editor would do. Those checks are yours.
